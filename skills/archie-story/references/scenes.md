# Scenes, correspondence, and voices

Read the participating characters' existing skills and dossiers before composing or reviewing their words. This reference governs scene craft and delivery; those files govern individual characterization.

## Establish the encounter

Know when and where the scene occurs, whose experience the player receives, who can perceive each action, and why the encounter happens now. Identify what each person wants from the other and the tactic they use: ask, correct, reassure, conceal, postpone, offer, challenge. A disagreement need not become an argument. Two people can want the same successful shoot and disagree about what evidence is enough.

Begin close enough to the pressure that the scene has a reason to exist. Let a response change the next action. End when the situation, expectation, or relationship has shifted sufficiently; a quiet encounter need not contain a revelation. If a passage feels inert, test its purpose and sequence before adding adjectives or a dramatic final line.

Keep the player within the intended viewpoint. An author may know why a ground panel is late; Tom knows only what he sees and what reaches him. A private letter from Rooke to Nell can be a useful author exercise, but it cannot appear on Tom's desk without an established route and reason.

## Write through the particular person

Use the dossier to decide what this person notices, leaves out, and expects the recipient to understand. Change the register with audience and circumstances. Do not distribute signature expressions, pauses, jokes, or qualifications mechanically.

The following are starting questions, not substitutes for the character skills:

| Character | A productive question | Common flattening to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Tom | What does he want this recipient to understand or do, and what part of his account is he reluctant to hand over? | Every letter becomes an upholstery metaphor or a polished confession |
| Vale | What grounds for his observation does the recipient need that he has left implicit? | Infallible mentor, permanent terseness, quotable lesson after every exchange |
| Pike | What useful difference did he notice, and how does being watched affect his report of it? | Helpless rookie, habitual stammering, decorative “sir,” repeated self-correction |
| Rooke | What actually arrived, who had to act on it, and what does the aircraft not know about the delay? | Omniscient ground narrator, comic relief, or self-appointed battery commander |

People can talk about home without rehearsing their military arc. Let family members have concerns that continue while the serviceman is away. Do not turn civilian life into a catalogue of symbols predicting the war.

For choices spoken or written by Tom, offer distinct intentions that remain plausible for him. The chosen text must fulfil the option's promise; a label meaning “ask for clarification” must not secretly deliver an accusation.

## Let the medium do some of the work

| Medium | Dramatic capacity | Practical limit |
|---|---|---|
| Spoken exchange | Immediate response, interruption, pressure, changed tactics | Establish who can hear whom; do not assume cockpit audibility or a radio reply |
| Chart or observer note | Contradictory marks, corrections, provenance, shared work | The player must be able to identify what changed and why it matters |
| Official log or Morse transcript | A durable record that can differ from someone's recollection | Preserve what was received; separate later annotations from the original entry |
| Personal letter | A selected account for a particular reader; delayed reply and domestic life | Date written, date received, and date read may differ; no instant knowledge across distance |
| Panel | Recognition, timing, a limited reply, an established unofficial variation | Cannot carry arbitrary paragraphs or an unexplained private meaning |
| Relayed telephone message | Compression, an intermediary's selection, news arriving indirectly | Identify source and route where needed; not a live airborne conversation |
| Object or photograph | Touch, use, ownership, absence, a relationship's physical trace | Establish how it reached the player and whether it can actually be inspected |
| Landscape or silence | Change in something previously familiar | Needs a baseline; ambiguity alone does not communicate a specific event |

For a letter, identify the occasion for writing and what the recipient already knows. Give it an ordinary practical life: answering a question, passing on news, enclosing something, arranging a matter. Let feeling affect selection and omission without obliging the writer to announce it. Vary length with purpose and available time. A long domestic letter can be right; a mission-critical instruction must be easy to locate.

For a log, be precise about observation versus inference. A personal annotation can complicate an official account without rewriting the recorded message. For a note in a margin, use the physical space and attachment to another document rather than writing a miniature speech.

## Objects and absence

Before using a keepsake, establish owner, giver, recipient, date, route, and association. Show care through handling, keeping, repairing, enclosing, or returning. The object does not need to be useful to remain valuable. Do not make every item fragile so it can be broken for emotion.

For the sixpence, consult the bible and both Tom's and Rooke's skills. It belongs to Rooke before first contact, travels after the first artillery sortie, and returns after the war. Tom's existing keepsake habit does not imply he has this particular coin at introduction. Pike has no automatic knowledge of its history when he joins.

An empty place or unanswered letter gains force from an earlier routine. Let the player make a supported inference; do not narrate an absence's meaning before they can notice it. Avoid treating every delayed reply as bereavement or making a missing person matter only through what their absence teaches Tom.

## Revise for ordinary speech and earned feeling

Read aloud, keeping audience and medium in mind. Remove explanations the recipient does not need, tidy rhetorical balances, automatic three-part lists, repeated trade metaphors, and final lines that sound written for a trailer. Keep an ordinary repeated noun when it is clearer than elegant variation.

Use standard spelling; accents are performance directions, not phonetic caricature. Verify distinctive period language when asserting authenticity. Avoid modern diagnostic or therapeutic vocabulary unless deliberately requested, while still allowing recognizably complex feelings.

Check subtext by naming privately what the character is trying to achieve. If every line says that intention explicitly, allow practical action or omission to carry some of it. If nothing is understandable without author explanation, add concrete context. Emotional restraint and obscurity are different problems.

When asked to make a scene more moving, try making the relationship, action, or consequence more particular before making the prose more lyrical. Preserve humour and unimportant details that let the people live beyond the plot.
