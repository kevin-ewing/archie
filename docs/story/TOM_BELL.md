# Tom Bell

**Reference point:** Rook Field, Flanders, May 1915, immediately before Tom is introduced at the barn desk. His first artillery-observation sortie still lies ahead. The trade, hometown, family names and other civilian particulars are the current workshop draft. Second Lieutenant is supported as a rank for an RFC pilot in 1915. His commission and training path remain open.

Historical basis and open particulars are tracked in the [historical research](../HISTORICAL_RESEARCH.md#cast-and-voice).

## Identity and early life

Thomas Bell is twenty-three in May 1915. He was born in autumn 1891 in Retford, Nottinghamshire. He goes by Tom. His working rank is Second Lieutenant. He is the pilot at Rook Field in Flanders. Arthur Vale is the experienced observer aboard his aircraft. Tom has yet to work with the battery in the field.

Tom grew up with his parents, his sister Edith and his younger brother Alfred. Their house is narrow and brick, with flags in the yard and a kitchen door that catches below the latch in wet weather. There is an apple tree against the back wall. His mother props up the low branch with a piece of wood. He knows which peg belongs to each coat and how far to lift the kitchen door to get it shut.

There was a regular wage in the house and little spare money. Tom left school in his mid-teens. He was comfortable with figures and could copy a list out cleanly. At school, he took particular care with the page he knew would be collected. His reluctance to hand over rough work dates from well before he flew.

## The upholstery shop

Tom went into an upholstery shop after school. He began with work at the bench and tasks the other men could check in passing. He learned to take out tacks without tearing the wood, keep fittings together and judge how much cloth remained before cutting. He liked the ordinary company of the shop and the way most jobs had a point in the day when everybody knew they were finished.

Before he enlisted, Tom was helping with money at home and looking at rooms he might take on his own. His place at the bench and his wage gave him a sense of what the next few years would look like. He left a chair he was working on for Alice at the shop. He had expected to finish it himself. As far as he knows, the chair is still at the shop with its seat unfinished.

He still takes care with the things he hands over. At the shop there was often time to straighten a row of tacks or redo a corner. He brings the same impulse to a chart. A chart can lose information when he makes it tidy. He learned to pick out landmarks as a pilot. His civilian work accounts for his habits with tools and unfinished work, and for the life he expected to have.

## The Bells

**Alice Bell, his mother, late forties.** Alice earns a little from alterations. She often sits working at the kitchen table after tea. Her letters tell him who came round, whose coat is waiting and who asked after him. Tom calls her Mother in writing and signs Tom. He pays attention to details that tell him how the house and garden are getting on. When Alice asks how he’s managing alone, he finds it easier to answer the questions about meals and sleep.

**George Bell, his father, early fifties.** George works on the goods side of the railway. He leaves the house in the dark and can find his coat by touch. He sometimes adds a practical question under Alice’s letter. He and Tom are at their ease when fixing something side by side. Tom has picked up George’s habit of giving directions from a place the other person will recognise. When Tom is worried about someone, he often begins by finding out whether there’s something they need.

**Edith Bell, his sister, nineteen.** Edie works as a clerk. She borrowed his books at home and put them back with scraps of paper between the pages. She knows the difference between what Tom says and what he goes to some trouble to leave out. He writes to her with less ceremony than to their parents. If she comes back to a question he’s ducked, he recognises the wording. He finds that harder to put off than a general inquiry after his health.

**Alfred Bell, his brother, thirteen.** Alf can draw a bicycle from memory. When Tom showed him how to draw the way from the house to school, Alf’s route turned out to cut through a series of other people’s yards. Tom kept looking at the drawing because Alf used all the little ways through that everybody at home knew. Tom takes Alf’s questions seriously. In letters to him, he explains things by their shape and where the parts go. Alf is impatient with anything that sounds like it came from a schoolbook.

## Leaving and flying

Tom enlisted early, while men around him were leaving. The pressure of going when they went sat alongside how badly he wanted to stay. He had already committed himself to the service by the time he reached Flanders in 1915. Exact recruitment circumstances and flight training are unrecorded in this dossier.

By his arrival at Rook Field he has become proud of being able to fly well enough to be given a job. He wants the squadron to trust him with work where the result matters. He pays attention when Vale points out a landmark on a chart or explains how he arrived at a bearing. Tom is still liable to hear a practical correction as a judgment on whether he was ready to be sent.

He’s learned to position the aircraft and pick out landmarks in flight. He approaches reporting as a matter of getting a clear observation to the right person. At this point he has little sense of what it takes the men at the battery to act on a report.

## Things he keeps and sends

In the Bell house, things passed between people all the time. Edie marked her place in Tom’s books with scraps from her day. George passed down tools with wear in the handles where his own fingers went. Tom kept a pencil stub George had handed him when they first repaired the kitchen door together. By the time it was too short to sharpen, he still knew which job it had come from. He had other pencils in the drawer.

Tom likes having something he can take out and look at when he wants to think about a person. A rough sketch in Alf’s hand is worth keeping even when Tom knows the place it shows. He remembers who gave him a thing, where they were and what it came wrapped in. He keeps letters with their envelopes and puts any enclosure back where he found it. A familiar mark on the paper can get his attention before he reads the words. He makes room for a few things that have used up their practical life.

He’s selective. At the shop he kept useful scraps by size so he could find them again. His own keepsakes are easier for him to place by person. Something bought new has to acquire its associations from use. Something handed over by a person he cares about can have them the moment he takes it. He’s private about the difference. If someone asks why he still has a worn-out pencil, he’s more likely to tell them whose it was than how it makes him feel.

Tom sends things, too. With Alf in particular, sending a drawing feels more like giving him something of his own time than writing several lines about what he’s been doing. He takes care over what he wraps and what he writes on the outside. He has a strong sense of who lent him something and who gave it to him outright. When he sends something back after keeping it a long while, he wants the person to see that it was looked after.

## Temperament and habits

Tom is at ease with a job whose end he can picture. He reads to the bottom of his orders early to see what he has to bring back. Then he starts from the beginning. He checks a pencil on the corner of the page with very little thought. When asked for a sheet he believes is good, he hands it over promptly. When he’s embarrassed about it, he keeps finding something to do to it.

He likes an exact answer. The prospect of giving people a doubtful answer feels to him like admitting he went out unprepared. If a chart contains two plausible marks, he’s inclined to settle on one before he hands the chart over. His desire to be useful can produce a clean report that gives another man less room to judge what he actually saw.

He does little practical things for people without drawing attention to them. Tools are easy for him to lend. An account of why he made a bad decision takes longer. He often asks a practical question before disagreeing and can sound as if he’s asking permission when he’s trying to hold his ground. When frightened and given time to think, he gets unusually polite. In an immediate difficulty he narrows his attention to the next action and needs fewer words.

He worries about burdening people at home. In a letter, he’ll give them the part of an anxious day they can do something with. He is more open about small discomforts than about loneliness. Around George, working on something together can make a long silence comfortable. Around people whose opinion he’s unsure of, he notices the silence and may try to fill it.

## Voice reference

**Accent and pace.** His speaking voice has a light Nottinghamshire sound. Standard spelling leaves the regional sound to a performer. He worked with customers as well as the men at the bench and is used to making himself easy to follow. At ease he speaks at an ordinary pace and gives people a chance to answer. Under immediate pressure the gap between noticing and saying a thing gets shorter. Anticipatory fear tends to make his speech more careful and formal.

**What goes first in a sentence.** In practical talk he often starts with the object or landmark the other person needs to find. He gives the direction once they both know where they’re looking. Given time, he tends to put an instruction or observation into a plain main clause and attach the reckoning, reason or exception after it. Shared context lets him drop a subject or leave a clause unfinished. When he corrects himself, he returns to the noun that caused the difficulty. A repeated noun feels ordinary to him.

**Vocabulary and idiom.** Everyday English, with tangible nouns and verbs of handling, movement and position. He speaks readily about what is under, beside, beyond, stuck, loose or worn. His vocabulary from the shop is precise when the object calls for it and otherwise stays in the background. He favours ordinary small qualifiers such as *a bit*, *a touch* and *a fair way*. He uses *I make it* for a reckoning, *that’ll do* to accept a workable result, and *you’d best* for a familiar warning. *I dare say* belongs with a domestic guess. Distinctive regional or period expressions **[VERIFY]**. He rarely reaches for an ornamental synonym when everyone already knows which thing he means.

**Address and register.** Tom speaks with contractions among family and familiar people. With a superior he gives a more complete sentence and uses the rank or ordinary form of address. He calls Arthur Vale by his surname in his working relationship. Exact military forms of address **[VERIFY]**. He begins with the ground contact’s working identification. At home he can address Alice as Mother and write to Edie by her familiar name. A recipient’s name is most useful to him when he needs their attention or has something unusually direct to say.

**Written rhythm.** He writes a log for a reader who needs a time and place, with what he saw set down plainly. It tends toward short entries and verifiable details. Family letters have more room. He may start with the thing just received, go to a household question and remember a message that needs passing on. His paragraphs can be uneven. Something small can take half the letter if it’s what he’s really sitting there thinking about. He can leave a difficult fact near the end and carry on to the signature.

**Humour and emotion.** His jokes arise from the thing everyone is dealing with. He understates a nuisance and sometimes lets somebody else decide whether it was a joke. Warmth shows in a remembered preference, an offer of help or an extra line to the person he nearly forgot to mention. He rarely supplies an explanation of his own feelings after the recipient has enough to understand him. He can be affectionate in a conventional closing and awkward in the body of the letter.

## Writing sample · a note to Edie about a chair at home

> Edie,
>
> The kitchen chair with the split rung is by the back door. I put it there so Father would see it and he’s hung his coat on it. Take the coat off before you say anything or he’ll have it that the chair is in use.
>
> Ask him if he’s got the little tin of tacks. I thought it was on the shelf with the jam jars. I looked last night and there’s a button in a saucer where the tin ought to be. The button’s off your blue coat, I think.
>
> I’ll be in after six. Leave the chair where it is.
>
> Tom
