# Will Rooke

**Reference point:** Flanders, 1915, immediately before Tom’s first artillery-observation sortie and the player’s first encounter with Rooke through the ground station. Rooke has the sixpence. He has had no correspondence with Tom. Name, family and civilian particulars are working draft details.

Historical basis and open particulars are tracked in the [historical research](../HISTORICAL_RESEARCH.md#cast-and-voice).

## Before the service

William Rooke is thirty-four. He grew up in a rented house in Grantham with his mother and his younger sister. His father was a house painter who took work where he could get it. Some weeks it brought him home every evening. During a long job away, he sent word through people who came back before he did. Will remembers learning to ask when the person had last seen his father. Somebody could be telling you quite accurately that he was well on Monday.

Will went to work young and eventually became a Post Office clerk. He worked the counter and learned telegraph work. Taking a message down accurately appealed to him. At the counter he also saw people wait to hear whether a name or address had gone through. A mistake might mean they had to come back when they could get off work again. Experience made him particular about what a sender had actually put down and who had to carry it the rest of the way. The period Post Office employed Sorting Clerks and Telegraphists. Will’s exact grade, his local office’s division of work and his training remain open.

He married Nell and rented a house close enough to walk to the office. He liked coming home after a late turn when the street was quiet, finding a plate set over his supper and being able to make his tea without waking anybody. He’d started setting money aside to buy a secondhand bicycle for Nell. He had found one that looked sound and been back to see it twice before he went away. The family was still discussing where she would keep it.

His service took him out of the work he understood and the ordinary arrangements he’d made at home. A great deal of what he knows about the household now comes from the space Nell can spare in a letter. His entry into service and his training dates are unrecorded here. Sergeant is the working rank. ## The people he writes to

**Eleanor “Nell” Rooke, his wife, thirty-two.** Nell takes in washing. She has a good memory for what someone said they’d pay and when they said they’d pay it. Will tries to do the household sums from the figures she sends him. She can tell him she’s already settled a matter and he’ll put a suggestion in his reply anyway. They enjoy each other’s company over small jobs. He can tell from the length of her account of a customer whether it was a nuisance or the best part of her day.

**James “Jim” Rooke, his son, six.** Jim likes the sort of errands where he gets to carry something in his own pocket. Before Will left, they had a turn around the streets after supper to see which shops still had their blinds up. Jim knew which grocer had a dog that could be seen through the bottom pane of the door. Will misses having this information brought to him by a boy who thinks it’s important. When he writes to Jim he leaves some room on the page for him to add something. Jim spells more confidently when there’s only one word to get down.

**Ada Rooke, his younger sister.** She works in a bakery and is a few streets from Nell. Will and Ada used to meet outside the shop after her shift when they were both walking home. Their idea of what it means to have helped somebody has a lot to do with making sure they got where they were going. Ada will send on a note if she knows it needs doing. Will sometimes sends one to her because he can trust her to choose the moment to put it into somebody’s hand.

## The man at the battery

Rooke is attached to the work of receiving airborne reports and passing them into the battery’s procedures. He deals with the people who have to carry an acknowledgement out into the open. He cares whether a report can be used as received and what a delay will cost the people waiting on it. He understands the effect his part of the job can have on a shoot. The battery’s command and firing decisions sit with the officers responsible for them.

He keeps a faithful record of what came through. If someone left out a detail, he wants the gap on the page before anyone supplies it from memory. If his own delay mattered, he writes down where it happened. For a man who has made an honest mistake, he’ll record what was happening around him. When the delay is his, he’s more likely to enter the time and leave everything else out.

He has spent much of his working life among people who could see why they were waiting. At the battery, he can be so occupied with sorting out a hold-up that he forgets what the silence looks like from the aircraft. He’s slow to send an explanation until he has a useful one. He can lose patience when someone in authority treats a trip across exposed ground as a line on a form. He knows who has to make that trip and roughly how long it will take. In a disagreement, he often settles on that man’s name and what he’s being asked to do.

## The sixpence and other small things

The sixpence was in the change his father brought home from a long job away. He gave it to Will on a morning when Will was old enough to go to the shop alone and young enough to think carefully about what he might spend it on. Will kept the money in a little tin until he had several coins in it. That was the one he could still identify by a nick at the edge. Later he had it drilled so he could keep it on cord. He took it with him when he left home. At the point we meet him, it’s still his.

Will associates the coin with the morning his father was home and there was time to go to the shop and back. He calls it lucky when the matter comes up. He seldom explains what he means. For himself he can be careless about a good pair of gloves and careful about a nicked coin. When he sends someone a small thing, he wraps it to survive the trip and gives the address as much attention as the note. He is liable to check later that the person got it.

## Voice reference

**Sound and pace.** A light Lincolnshire inflection is the working direction. Write ordinary spelling and leave local pronunciation to performance. At a counter or on duty he’s used to people hearing part of a sentence through other noise. He puts the important detail where it can be heard, then gives the other person room to repeat it. A pause is ordinary to him. In private talk he can wait long enough for somebody to answer properly.

**Order of information.** When accuracy matters, he starts with what arrived and in what state. He puts his explanation below or alongside it after the reader has the entry itself. When he writes a personal note, a detail from the edge of the event can come first. He often knows where someone was standing or who had to go back. He expects a reader to understand why that person mattered once the action is on the page.

**Vocabulary and idiom.** He favours ordinary verbs of receipt, delivery and handling. The distinctions between sending, getting and passing something on matter to him. A time expression usually refers to when a thing was done or when he last knew it to be true. He uses *word* for news passed from a person, *come to hand* occasionally in a formal note, and *by the time* when the delay is what he wants to tell you about. He repeats a name or an address when clarity calls for it. Distinctive period and regional usage **[VERIFY]**.

**Sentence shape.** The duty record favours short declarative units, with the responsible person or source made clear where known. His notes have more give. He can state the business in one line and take a longer sentence to tell what happened in getting it done. He’ll add a corrective detail the next time he picks up the pencil. He often leaves the first account standing when the added detail belongs to a different part of the story.

**Audience and address.** A report is for whoever takes the next watch or acts on the message. A margin note is for the person whose day depended on the entry. At home he writes to Nell as a person with an equal share in the question. He can address Jim with careful instructions and leave him a small responsibility he can actually manage. Military forms of address depend on the ranks and affiliation we establish. The exact wording of signals and reports **[VERIFY]**.

**Humour and feeling.** His humour can come from the length of time a simple thing took or from what an animal or child did while the adults were organising it. He includes his own difficulty if it’s part of what made the business ridiculous. When he is fond of someone, he pays attention to whether they got what they were expecting. He can fit affection into a workaday request and leave it there. His temper is sharpest when another person’s work has been made invisible by the way a thing is described.

## Writing sample · a note to Nell about a parcel

> Nell,
>
> The grocer brought the parcel while you were at Ada’s. I put it on the settle. The brown paper has come open at the bottom. If you lift it by the string you’ll have the whole lot on the floor, which is how I came to find out.
>
> Jim has put the small tin in the pantry for you. He wanted to give it to you himself, so if he asks where it is you might let him fetch it.
>
> They’ve charged us for the large tin. I’ll take the bill in on my way past.
>
> Will
