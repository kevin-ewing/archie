# Arthur Vale

**Reference point:** Flanders, 1915, immediately before the player first encounters Vale at Rook Field. He has been assigned as Tom Bell’s observer. They have yet to fly their first artillery sortie together. His civilian particulars are a working draft.

Historical basis and open particulars are tracked in the [historical research](../HISTORICAL_RESEARCH.md#cast-and-voice).

## The life he had

Arthur Vale is thirty-six. He grew up outside Derby, the elder of two children. His father repaired shoes from a bench in the front room. The customer stood in the doorway while his father turned a boot over and decided whether it would take another sole. Arthur used to write the names on slips and put each pair together on the shelf. His mother did the household accounts. When the sums wanted looking at again, she called him over and left the book open between them.

Vale went into railway surveying as a young man. He began by carrying equipment, keeping the field book and copying measurements. By the time he left for the service, he was accustomed to going out in charge of a small party. He knew the men’s ways of working and could tell from the entries who’d held the staff and who’d written. He liked fieldwork, especially its quiet stretches when everyone knew what to get on with. Long days sometimes ended with a cold meal and a walk from the station because he had missed the last lift home.

One of his first notebooks was soaked through. Back in the office he copied the legible figures, filled an empty line from memory and left the copy looking as though it all came from the same place. His foreman found that line still clean in the wet book and sent him back out. Vale remembers the wasted morning and the man who had to come with him. He now keeps a rough sheet with its corrections and records what a figure came from. He is quick to identify missing evidence in a piece of work. He sometimes forgets to show the other person how he identified it.

His railway work was a livelihood shared with men he knew well. At home he could tell where he was in the week from what was laid out for breakfast. Service took him out of both routines. He still thinks of the railway office as the office, even when writing from another one. ## Home

**Margaret Vale, his wife, thirty-four.** Margaret worked in a stationer’s shop before they married. She still knows when a book has been ruled badly and turns a sheet over to use the clean side. They rent a house with a box room that Arthur has tried to make into a place to work. She knows his habit of solving the wrong problem because it’s the one he can get his hands on. In letters to her he gets to the business early, then goes back and adds the part he wanted to tell her. He can leave practical matters to her with a confidence he rarely shows when delegating at work.

**Ruth Vale, his daughter, eight.** Ruth reads notices aloud and wants to know who put them up. Arthur used to draw little diagrams in the margins of the household paper when she asked how something worked. She asks him back when one leaves out the part she cares about. He keeps a paper ruler she drew for him. Its divisions get progressively farther apart and she put his name across the top. In his field book he requires a true measure. He keeps her paper folded into the back where he can find it.

**Ellen Vale, his younger sister.** Ellen still lives near their mother and helps with her shopping. She tells Arthur when someone has actually asked for his help. He has a habit of writing a second letter with instructions nobody requested. Ellen has been known to send the first one round to the person who needed it and keep the second for her own amusement. He trusts her to tell him what she thinks.

Vale takes his domestic responsibilities seriously. It matters to him that Margaret can spend money without writing to ask him first. He keeps estimates, receipts and addresses together because someone else may have to find them. At home he can spend a good part of an evening getting a drawer to run properly. He’s capable of leaving a letter unanswered while the drawer is on the table.

## The observer before we meet him

Vale has more experience observing from the air than Bell. He’s used to looking again at a place from a different angle and recording the basis for an identification. He notices what part of a landmark will actually show first. From above, the gap in a hedge can identify the field a little sooner than the hedge itself. He wants an observation another man can use after he has stopped being there to explain it.

He wants Bell to fly the aircraft with enough room to make his own judgment. His instincts can get in the way. When a man is slow to arrive at an answer, Vale tends to supply it. He sometimes redoes work quietly to save the person embarrassment. That leaves the person a sound copy and very little idea what was wrong with the first. He has been on both sides of this exchange and still finds it easier to fix the thing than to talk through the mistake.

He leaves room for doubt on his own rough sheets. Under pressure he’ll mark what he saw and what he has to take on an assumption. In conversation he can leave the assumption unstated because he’s already accounted for it in his head. A listener may hear a flat instruction and miss that there was a judgment behind it. Once shown that a mark is his own mistake, he wants the correction on the copy people actually use, where it can still do some good.

When he’s irritated, he speaks more exactly. When he’s worried about someone, he attends to what they’ll have to do in the next few minutes. He can be generous with his time for practical work. He gets impatient with people who claim to be ready so they can finish a conversation. He has more patience with somebody who asks a plain question, even if they ought to know the answer by now.

## Things and correspondence

Vale writes a name or a date on the things another person may need to identify. He lends tools in working order and checks them before putting them away. Wear usually tells him how something has been used. A stain in the wrong place can tell him a paper has been handled in the rain. He remembers who looked after a thing well.

What he keeps for himself is often tucked into something he uses every day. Ruth’s ruler is there when he needs his book. He has kept it through more than one change of working papers. He straightens its fold with his thumb when he opens it. The few personal things he takes care to carry have little to do with what he thinks somebody back at home should spend money on. When Margaret sends something useful, he tells her when it arrived and what he’s done with it.

## Voice reference

**Sound and pace.** A light Derbyshire inflection is the working performance direction. Write ordinary spelling. Vale speaks at a steady pace when he has had time to look. If someone puts two questions together, he often answers the one that has to be settled first and takes the other afterward. When interrupted he can stop cleanly in the middle of an explanation. If the answer still matters, he goes back to the point where he was interrupted.

**Order of information.** Vale tends to establish where an observation came from before asking somebody else to rely on it. In a written correction he puts the location, date or source where the eye will find it quickly. If he has to explain the correction aloud, he can start with the point at issue and supply the grounds in one short clause. He uses a qualification when it changes what a reader can do with the information. He often leaves routine background implicit. With a learner, those omissions can make an instruction harder to follow.

**Vocabulary and idiom.** His words are plain and particular. He favours verbs of checking, setting, holding, measuring, rubbing out and entering. He distinguishes an estimate from a measurement in his choice of verbs. He’ll specify which copy or which side of a thing if there is actual room for confusion. He uses *as far as I could see* to limit an observation, *for the present* for an arrangement he considers temporary, and *see to* for work he has actually taken on. Such phrases are occasional. **Sentence shape.** There is usually a main statement he wants to get down before anything else. Brief operational exchanges can be fragments. His explanations have more ordinary conjunctions and longer runs when several things really depend on one another. When he changes his mind partway through, he can correct one word and carry on. He tends to use the same term for an object from one sentence to the next. He sounds more human when an incidental detail takes him slightly off course and he has to return to the business.

**To different people.** On a work sheet he can sound abrupt. He assumes the person reading it is busy and puts requests where they can be acted on. With Margaret he tells her where he left things and may include an unnecessary domestic detail because the detail amuses him. With Ruth he takes more time. He follows the order of her question and uses familiar objects when he has to explain a part she hasn’t seen. In official writing he records the extent of his responsibility. Domestic writing gives him more room to say what he remembered on the way home or meant to get done before tea.

**Humour and affection.** He can give an object an unearned dignity in a dry remark, particularly a badly made item of furniture or a tool that keeps turning up in the wrong drawer. He seldom pauses to see whether it landed. Affection often shows in the room he makes for someone to do a thing in their own way. In a private letter an acknowledgement of what the other person has already done can be more generous than anything he says at the table. He can let a plain sign-off stand after a paragraph about nothing momentous.

## Writing sample · a note to Margaret about the apples

> Margaret,
>
> The apples in the shallow box are the ones from under the wall. Have a look at the bottom layer before you put the box in the pantry. Two had soft places this morning. I left those on a plate and Ruth took one before I had the other cut up.
>
> I put the good paper under the box to keep it from rocking. I remembered what it was after I’d set the box down. There is a clean piece still in the drawer, beneath the string.
>
> If you go to Ellen’s before I get back, take her some of the apples out of the basket. I’ve already told her she can have the basket. I had forgotten you wanted it Saturday.
>
> Arthur
