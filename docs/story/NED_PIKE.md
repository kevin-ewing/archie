# Ned Pike

**Reference point:** The Somme, summer 1916, immediately before the player first meets Ned. He has been assigned as Tom Bell’s new observer and has yet to fly with him. His family, hometown and civilian history are workshop draft details.

Historical basis and open particulars are tracked in the [historical research](../HISTORICAL_RESEARCH.md#cast-and-voice).

## Before the service

Edward Pike is twenty. His family calls him Ned. He grew up over a small jobbing print shop in Leicester. The bell on the shop door could be heard upstairs. Sometimes his mother would set a plate over his supper while he went down to see what somebody wanted. The shop had a side door to the yard, where he kept his bicycle. He spent a fair amount of his wages getting it into better condition than anybody else thought it needed.

After leaving school he worked in the shop. He delivered parcels, helped fold and count jobs, and eventually took on small orders. He liked having one that was his from receipt to delivery. At first it took somebody else’s initials for a job to go out. He watched for the point at which his father or aunt stopped asking to see it before he wrapped it.

He remembers a customer bringing back a bundle of sale notices with the wrong closing date. Ned had copied the date from the previous year’s notice. The new date had been pencilled on the back of the order. He’d seen the pencil mark and taken it for something already dealt with. He spent the morning going back round the shops where he’d delivered the old notices. A few had already put them in the window. He paid close attention from then on to what had changed between two versions. He also acquired a fear of looking slow while somebody stood over him waiting for a decision.

Before the war, he had started to imagine what he would buy for the shop if he had money of his own to put into it. He liked riding home after a delivery with nobody expecting him for twenty minutes. The shop is still going without him. He has had letters about changes to the counter and the hours. He can picture the old arrangement better than the new one.

When he left for service, his aunt started doing a round he’d done on the bicycle. His sister took on some of the work he’d done downstairs. He wants them to manage and finds the evidence that they can a little hard to take. His route into service, training dates and rank are still open for historical verification. He has had instruction and practice as an observer by the time we meet him. His experience at this point is chiefly instructional, and the sight picture over the Somme is new to him.

## The Pikes

**Edwin Pike, his father, mid-forties.** Runs the shop with his sister. He has a bad thumb that makes tying packets slow in cold weather. He lets Ned finish a job if Ned started it, even if he could do the last bit quicker himself. He rarely gives praise as directly as Ned would like. Being allowed to wrap something and send it out counted for more than being told it looked well. Ned is still inclined to listen for permission to proceed when somebody older has already left the work to him.

**Mabel Pike, his mother, mid-forties.** Keeps the household going and takes orders when the others are occupied. She remembers customers by what brought them in. Ned addresses her as Mother and uses her as his audience for things he wants the rest of the house to hear. She asks him to put the important answer where she can find it on the first page. In a letter home he can spend a good deal of time setting out the part of a story she would have asked him to get past.

**Elsie Pike, his sister, sixteen.** Handles more of the counter since Ned went away. She can do arithmetic in her head while listening to someone who keeps changing the order. He is proud of it and annoyed when she corrects him in front of anyone. They lend each other money in small amounts and each remembers a different balance. She writes what she thinks he’ll find funny. He writes her details he would cut from a letter addressed to the whole household.

**Ada Pike, his aunt.** Works the shop with Edwin. She was the first to let Ned handle a customer who had come back about a mistake. She stayed at the other end of the counter and made him do the talking. He remembers the relief of hearing her go on with her own work while he dealt with it. He also remembers how long it took him to recognise that she’d done it on purpose.

## The observer before we meet him

Ned wants to be trusted with a piece of the job that can be his to finish. His immediate measure of trust is whether Tom will make another circuit or alter course on his word. He is good at noticing where two views fail to agree. If a shape, opening or name has shifted, it can catch his attention before he has sorted out why it matters. He still has to learn what to make of those differences from the air.

He works quickly when someone is waiting. Silence can feel like a request for a better answer. He’ll offer the best one he has while he is still checking it, then find it difficult to interrupt with a doubt he should have made clear at the start. When copying his own work, he straightens names and takes out marks he no longer thinks he needs. A question mark can disappear on that second pass while the doubt that produced it is still there.

When left enough time to make a second look, he uses it well. He’s willing to go back through his work in private. Being watched changes what he thinks the work is for. Part of his attention goes to whether he’s looking like someone who can do it. He learns quickly from a specific question. General praise makes him pleased and leaves him guessing which part he got right.

Ned likes people with whom he can be useful without asking first. He’ll offer to carry a parcel, then forget to ask where it’s going. A brusque reply can make him unusually formal for the next few minutes. He usually wants a chance to try again and can wait longer for it if he knows what he got wrong.

## Things he takes with him

He brought a small folding packet of paper with him, made from offcuts Elsie saved when he left. Some pieces are better for writing than others. He keeps the packet inside a book to stop the corners curling, takes from the middle and puts the scraps with something on them back in sideways. He likes using things from home. The packet thins as he writes and sends pieces away.

He gives things a second life readily. At home, a bit of paper with enough blank space became a label or a note. He pays attention to an address when he has to get something to a person. Letters from home get folded up and read again. A funny single line can matter to him more than the long account around it. He hasn’t met Vale and has no personal link to the objects or correspondence already in Tom’s keeping.

## Voice reference

**Sound and pace.** A light Leicester inflection is the working performance direction. Use ordinary spelling. Ned starts quickly when he feels a pause opening up. If he remembers the next part before the sentence has finished, he can add it with an ordinary conjunction and keep going. When at ease with Elsie, that speed can be infectious. With a senior man he sometimes begins very distinctly, as though reading the first line out. The rest speeds up once he has got into it.

**Sentence shape.** In speech the provisional answer can come first. The observation and any exception catch up after it. When he thinks somebody has misunderstood, he adds a detail immediately and may change the order in which he tells the whole thing. His more formal sentences often begin as complete written constructions and loosen halfway through. When concentrating, he can use short phrases with a pause between them. Save those pauses for moments when he’s actually looking or deciding.

**Vocabulary and idiom.** His words come readily for differences he can point to, including where something ends, how far it runs or which side it was on the last time. He can use precise shop words about type or paper when speaking to family. In the air his vocabulary is mostly ordinary, and a word from the shop only belongs if it is one he’d genuinely reach for in the moment. He uses *I thought* when going back over a first impression, *only* when inserting the detail that changes it, and *I mean* when he’s repairing an account already under way. Those turns are occasional, especially in a hurried exchange. Period-specific terms and regional usage **[VERIFY]**.

**Register and address.** Ned leans formal with someone who can judge his work. His use of *sir* can arrive at the end of a sentence he started too freely. He uses Bell’s surname when speaking about him to someone else, subject to the ranks and forms of address we establish. With Elsie, he uses contractions, old references and fewer explanations. He can start a letter with a businesslike request and get caught up in a detail he wants her to picture.

**How he writes.** He likes a straight margin and a legible heading. A first draft can start answer-first and add the reason lower down. On the clean sheet he may move the reason up or take out the marks that made the page look hesitant. In a personal letter he has less reason to copy it again. That is where his changes of direction and late qualifications come through. When something annoys or amuses him, he often reports who was standing there. He knows that the person at home will want to know.

**Humour and feeling.** A private joke often comes from a small injustice that matters to him just long enough to be funny. He remembers what Elsie will dispute and may include a detail solely because she’ll come back at him over it. Embarrassment can make him sound officious. When he is genuinely upset and trusts the person, he writes more plainly and can leave the explanation incomplete. He likes a definite answer to a small question from home because it gives him something to write back about.

## Writing sample · a note to Elsie about the bicycle

> Elsie,
>
> You can take my bicycle on Saturday. Father said you were going to borrow Ada’s, but her back tyre was soft when I put it away and she says the pump lets more out than it puts in. Mine is against the yard wall, behind the sack.
>
> The bell sounds if you pull the lever out a little first. I did tell Father that. He tried it with his gloves on and said it was broken.
>
> If you go past the shop with the postcards in the window, see if they’ve still got the one of the dog on the chair. It was there Tuesday. I went back for it after tea and they’d shut.
>
> Ned
