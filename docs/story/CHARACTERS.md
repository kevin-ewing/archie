# Archie character workshop

First pass. The jobs and chapter placements come from the workshop decisions. Ages, names, backgrounds, sample lines and turning points are proposals.

**Question to test across the cast.** When men on the ground are waiting on your word, how much certainty do you owe them? The pilot wants to sound sure. The observer has learned to qualify what he sees. The rookie wants to be trusted with the call. The artillery contact has to act on whatever reaches him.

## The older observer · Flanders, 1915

[Arthur Vale’s opening dossier and voice reference](ARTHUR_VALE.md). The notes below are campaign workshop material.

**Working name.** Arthur Vale, 36. A railway surveyor before the war. He can orient himself from a cutting, drainage ditch or hedge gap. He writes the bearings down because experience has taught him how badly memory edits a difficult flight.

**What he wants.** To make their reports dependable enough for the guns to use, and to bring the pilot back with the confidence to make his own calls.

**What gets him into trouble.** He corrects people before he explains himself. He thinks he is saving time. From the front seat it can feel as though he has already decided the pilot will get it wrong. He’ll redo a junior man’s chart in private, which means the junior man never sees the part he needed help with.

**What changes.** Early on he hands over a finished chart. Later there are two possible marks on it, with his doubts written alongside them. He gives the pilot enough information to disagree and backs him when the disagreement costs time.

**First impression.** Two versions of a route lie on the crate. Vale has marked a hedge on one and circled a gap in it on the other. “You’ll see the gap first. I’ve marked both.”

**Pressure and choices.** During a confused shoot, Vale realizes that one of his own marks is wrong. He crosses it out in the copy that goes to the battery and signs the correction. The pilot gets to decide how they account for the delay in the sortie report.

**Voice on paper.** Exact, dry, sparing with reassurance. The care shows up in practical things. Draft margin note: “The slate is in your pocket. The dry pencil is in mine. Take both next time.”

**Departure.** Working version is an instructor posting at the chapter turn. His last chart has room for the rookie’s name and only one note addressed to the pilot. The injury version would put more weight on what Vale can bring himself to write after he loses his place in the aircraft.

## The rookie observer · Somme, 1916

[Ned Pike’s opening dossier and voice reference](NED_PIKE.md). The notes below are campaign workshop material.

**Working name.** Edward “Ned” Pike, 20. Grew up around a print shop. He has sharp eyes for what has changed on a familiar page, including things the pilot skims past. He copies orders quickly and has a bad habit of tidying away the question marks.

**What he wants.** To be given a piece of the job the pilot will trust him to finish. He wants his observations to carry enough weight that the pilot will change course on his word.

**What gets him into trouble.** Silence makes him think he has disappointed someone. He fills it with a quick answer, then spends the next few minutes trying to make that answer hold up. He is good at noticing small changes, even while he is still learning which ones matter.

**What changes.** At first he hands over a clean copy with a doubtful mark. Later he challenges an identification when he has a good reason, and shows his working so the pilot can decide whether to act on it.

**First impression.** The pilot finds a meticulously copied map with a village name written on the wrong bank. In the air, Ned spots that a roof has gone missing since the map was drawn. That observation is what eventually settles where they are.

**Pressure and choices.** The battery is waiting. Ned wants a second pass before he puts his name to a target. A pilot who gives him time gets a better mark and may lose light. A pilot who keeps moving still gets Ned’s written account afterward, which can change how they work the next sortie.

**Voice on paper.** Over-formal in orders, quick and concrete in the margins. Draft line: “I have put the wood back where it belongs. The church is what I’m doubtful about now.”

**Workshop.** What part of observation does Pike take pride in first? Give him one occasion to see something the player and Vale would both miss.

## The artillery contact · on the ground

[Will Rooke’s opening dossier and voice reference](WILL_ROOKE.md). The notes below are campaign workshop material.

**Working name.** Sergeant Will Rooke, 34. Rename him or Rook Field if the similarity implies a connection we don’t want. A former Post Office clerk who prefers a legible incoming message to a clever one. The working job is battery wireless and liaison. His service, rank, responsibilities, and authority over firing or panels **[VERIFY]**.

**What he wants.** Enough information to get the battery through a shoot without wasting rounds or taking a risk based on a bad report. He also wants his own work and the men doing it to be visible in the squadron’s account.

**What gets him into trouble.** He treats a silence as a delay he can manage, right up to the point the pilot starts to think it means something worse. His logs are generous about other people’s mistakes and merciless about his own. He has a temper when an officer describes moving cloth in an exposed position as a simple job.

**What changes.** He gradually gives the pilot more context about what a request costs down there. At a turn in the relationship he volunteers why an acknowledgement took so long, a detail he would once have left out of the official line.

**First impression.** The player gets the expected acknowledgement from a panel. The next morning, the transcript slips out of the envelope with an extra line in pencil: “Had to send the runner back for the corner. Wind up here too.” The pilot can learn the man’s habits before learning much about his life.

**Pressure and choices.** The ground station has an uncertain readback and the guns are waiting. He presses for confirmation through the available procedure or asks the battery to hold. In a later letter he owns the delay. His decision should affect the timing of the shoot, giving him agency even though we never cut to his point of view.

**Voice on paper.** Practical, observant and occasionally funny at his own expense. He puts official information in the body and personal information at the edge. Draft line: “The panel was straight when we put it out. By the time you saw it, the goat had formed a view.”

**Workshop.** Keeping him in both chapters gives the player one familiar relationship while the landscape and crewmate change. Leaving him in Flanders gives letters a new weight and makes the Somme feel lonelier. Decide which feeling we want on the first 1916 sortie.

## The relationships to test first

| Pair | What you can put in a scene |
|---|---|
| Pilot and older observer | Vale offers two possible marks. Bell wants one before he sends the report. |
| Pilot and rookie | Bell recognizes his old habit in the rookie’s clean chart. The rookie still has information Bell needs. |
| Pilot and ground contact | Rooke writes down what the pilot actually sent. The pilot learns how a confident transmission sounded to the people taking it down. |
| Older observer and rookie | They can share a chart without sharing a scene. Vale leaves a corrected mark. In the Somme, the rookie adds a third reference the experienced men overlooked. |

The Major and Recording Officer can carry orders and official accounts in the first pass. Give either a full profile once we have a scene where their decision changes what the crew can do.
